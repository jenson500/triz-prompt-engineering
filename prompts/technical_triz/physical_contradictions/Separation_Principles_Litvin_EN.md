# Resolving Physical Contradictions — Litvin Variant (EN)

Source of the strategy/principle assignment: **S. Litvin, 1993**.

This variant offers **three methods of resolution**: *Separation*, *Satisfy* and *Bypass*.
The method *Separation* is subdivided into **four separation principles**.

> **Terminology rule:** In English always use exactly these terms: *Separation in space*,
> *Separation in time*, *Separation in relation*, *Separation in system level*, *Satisfy*, *Bypass*.
> Do not mix them with the terms of the Zlotin/Zusman variant
> (see `Separation_Principles_Zlotin_Zusman_EN.md`).

## 2. Working order

Work through the six approaches **in the order given above**. If one approach yields no
solution, move on to the next. Document ideas for every approach that is applicable.

Reasons for this order:

* For the first two approaches it is comparatively easy to check whether they are applicable at all.
* Separation in time usually produces somewhat more complex solutions than separation in space,
  because it requires some form of dynamization of the system — therefore check space first.
* Separation in relation and separation in system level often lead to very intelligent solutions,
  but finding them requires considerably more thinking.
* Bypass is a last resort: it requires a change of the operating principle, i.e. usually a rather
  fundamental change of the engineering system.

**Additionally:** the recommended principles are short-cuts, not a restriction. It is always
permissible to search across **all 40 Inventive Principles** for further ideas.

## 3. Conditions of applicability

* **Separation in space** and **separation in time** are applicable exactly when the places or the
  periods of time in which the contradictory requirements apply **do not overlap**. If they do
  overlap, the separation principle cannot be applied, no matter how much ingenuity is invested.
* **Separation in relation** presupposes that the justifications of the two contradictory
  requirements originate from **two different components**. This is not sufficient on its own — a
  usable property in which these two components differ must also be found. This is not always
  possible and usually requires creativity.
* **Separation in system level** is the only principle that can always be applied in principle.
  However, deriving a concrete solution from it is comparatively rare and often demands great
  intellectual effort.

**The justifications are part of the contradiction.** A physical contradiction without both
justifications is incomplete, because the justifications decide which separation principle is
applicable at all. If a justification is missing, ask the user for it.

A justification may take one of two forms:

1. A **goal or requirement** that must be achieved when solving the problem or further developing
   the system. If the parameter had that value, this goal would be met.
2. A **law of nature or a typical property**, i.e. a fundamental characteristic of a component
   dictated by the laws of nature or by inherent properties of materials and systems.

A physical contradiction in which one of the two requirements is not justified by an objective is
not a contradiction — it points to a solution.

## 4. The methods in detail

### 4.1 Separation in space

Applicable if the two contradictory requirements for the same property have to be fulfilled only
in **different, non-overlapping regions of space**.

*Example (applicable):* A garage should be closed so that the car does not get wet in the rain, and
a garage should be open so that you can easily drive in. Marking the regions where "closed" and
"open" are required shows that they do not overlap — a **carport** resolves the contradiction.

*Example (not applicable):* A garage should be closed so that no thief can get to the car, and it
should be open so that you can easily drive in. The requirements are identical to the example
above, but the justifications differ — now the regions overlap, separation in space is impossible
and the carport is no longer a solution.

*Tip:* Visualise or draw as many views of the situation as possible. Two intersecting traffic
flows overlap in a top view, but a side view shows that the overlap can be removed by deforming
the roads — hence a bridge or tunnel.

### 4.2 Separation in time

Applicable if the two contradictory requirements have to be fulfilled only in **different,
disjoint periods of time**.

*Example (applicable):* A swimming pool should be filled with water so that you can swim in it, and
it should not be filled with water because freezing water damages the pool below 0 °C. People swim
in summer, frost damage occurs in winter — fill in May, drain in September.

*Example (not applicable):* A swimming pool should be filled with water so that you can swim in it,
and it should not be filled with water so that small children cannot fall in and drown.

*Example (different but overlapping — therefore not applicable):* A house wall should have an
opening so that people can walk through, and it should not have an opening so that no cold wind
gets in. The opening is only needed when somebody enters or leaves, but heat loss is undesirable at
all times: the periods differ, yet they overlap. An ordinary door is therefore only a compromise,
not a full resolution — while the door is open, cold air does enter.

### 4.3 Separation in relation

Applicable if the two justifications refer to **different components** (components of the
super-system included) and a usable distinguishing property between them can be found. Because the
distinction is often exploited by a change of state or condition, this principle is also referred
to as *separation by change of condition*.

*Examples:* The front door should be closed so that no thief can get in, and it should be open
because otherwise the dog will do its business in the living room → a dog flap exploits the
difference in body size. — The outside wall should be open so that sunlight can get in, and it
should be closed to keep the heat in → a pane of glass is open for the electromagnetic field and
closed for the air molecules that carry convective heat.

*Example (temporary change of state):* In sandblasting, solid particles remove the top layer of the
workpiece but contaminate it and endanger the operator's health. Contradiction: the particles in
the air stream should be solid so that enough material is removed, and they should not be solid so
as not to leave any dirt behind. Solution: frozen carbon dioxide — solid on impact, then it
evaporates without residue.

*Counter-example:* The front door should be closed so that no thief can get in, and it should be
open so that all friends and acquaintances can come in. No usable distinguishing feature exists
(keys and passwords do not resolve it, since a thief may obtain them) — separation in relation is
not possible here.

### 4.4 Separation in system level

Two contradictory requirements are made compatible by fulfilling them on **different system
levels**: the system behaves differently on different length scales, or the system has a different
property than its super-system or one of its sub-systems.

*Examples:* A belt for power transmission should be soft so that it can be guided around the drive
wheel, and hard so that it can transmit a higher force → a **chain**: the chain links are hard,
the chain as a whole is flexible. — Four intersecting road sections should have an intersection so
that you can change from one road section to another, and they should not have an intersection
because intersections are dangerous → a **roundabout**: from a distance indistinguishable from a
junction, close up it consists only of safer junctions.

### 4.5 Satisfy

Both requirements are met — as with separation — but now **simultaneously**. Recommendations are
often related to smart materials and scientific effects. Frequently a deeper look reveals that the
two requirements refer to *almost* but not *exactly* the same property.

*Example:* A running track should be long so that you can run reasonably on it, and it should be
short so that it fits into a small enclosed space. "Short" refers to the length in a stationary
frame of reference, "long" to the length relative to the runner, i.e. in a moving frame — a
**treadmill** satisfies both requirements.

### 4.6 Bypass

The engineering system is changed so that the physical contradiction **becomes irrelevant** and no
longer needs to be resolved — e.g. by finding a different way of performing the desired function or
by changing the operating principle. Usually applied when separation and satisfaction fail.

*Examples:* The boiler wall of a steam locomotive should be thick so that a high steam pressure can
be used, and thin so that the locomotive is light. The contradiction follows from the operating
principle "steam"; switching to electric or diesel traction makes it invalid. — A drill should
rotate quickly so that the hole is clean, and slowly so that it does not overheat; laser drilling
or water-jet cutting makes the contradiction meaningless.

## 5. How to use this file

1. Formulate the challenge as a physical contradiction, **including a justification for each of the
   two values** of the parameter. (Engineering contradictions can be converted into physical
   contradictions beforehand.)
2. Go through the six methods in the order of section 2 and check applicability using section 3.
3. For each applicable method, look up the recommended Inventive Principles from section 1 in
   `40_Inventive_Principles_EN.md` and derive concrete solution ideas from them.
4. Optionally continue the search across all 40 Inventive Principles.
5. Write down the concrete ideas for every abstract solution direction.
